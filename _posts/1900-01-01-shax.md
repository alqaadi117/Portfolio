# Product description

Shax (also known as Jar) is an abstract strategy game with custodian capture rules, played on a 5X5 board. The game consists of two phases: a _placement phase_ followed by a _movement and capture phase_. Shax is popular in East Africa, and while its exact origin is unknown, it is closely related to _Seega_, a traditional game from Egypt.

This demo showcases an AI engine for Shax, implemented using Monte Carlos tree search algorithm. Rather than solving the game exhaustively, the engine optimally traverses through the nodes to identify strong moves. The demo also includes visualization of the moves explored by MCTS with metadata attached to individual nodes.

## MCTS


Game decisions form a branching search space, too large to fully calculate. A **greedy policy** takes the immediate best reward but often fails long term. **Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS)** tackles this by running **simulated rollouts** and updating **value estimates** at each branch. Its key innovation is balancing **exploration and exploitation**, much like modern AI agents do when learning from experience. Over many iterations, the search **converges toward optimal strategies** without needing prior training data.

In small games like tic-tac-toe, the state space is tiny enough that the full game tree can be pre-computed. But in larger games that scale toward **PSPACE-hard** or even **EXPTIME-level** complexity, the branching and depth explode, making exhaustive search impossible. This is where **Monte Carlo Tree Search** proves valuable: by running targeted simulations, it can handle decision spaces far beyond what brute-force methods allow.

### [gpt version]<br>
Several challenges arose during the development of the engine. Initially, the absence of simulation in the MCTS resulted in moves that were predictable and uninspiring, with reduced entropy. In some cases, the AI became overly defensive, while in others it neglected potential dangers; this inconsistency stemmed from the lack of bi-turn backpropagation. The most difficult issue, however, was out-of-memory (OOM) errors. In this section, each of these problems will be discussed in detail.

During the early stages of development, the AI often played too defensively because all stages of backpropagation were weighted equally. This issue was resolved by applying exponential decay along the backpropagation path. Conversely, when the AI played too aggressively, the problem stemmed from nodes that ended prematurely, without completing a full bi-turn cycle. For example, after a sequence like _AI → Human → AI_, the engine mistakenly assumed an advantage, without accounting for the opponent’s next move. This significantly hindered performance. The solution was to restrict backpropagation to even-numbered moves, ensuring that evaluations always reflected complete cycles.<br>

<img src="{{ site.baseurl }}/assets/Bi-turn.svg" style="display: block; margin: 0 auto;">

Out-of-memory (OOM) exceptions were a recurring problem in the web build of the engine, primarily because AI algorithms demand substantial memory. The jar-engine in particular experienced spikes of up to 1.5 GB after each calculation. This occurred because the heuristic function used ten `List<int[2]>`, each containing four `int[2]`. Since the heuristic was invoked five times per node across 30,000 nodes, the process required approximately six million heap allocations, leading to severe memory overhead.

To address this, two optimizations were introduced. First, lists were replaced with structs wherever possible to avoid repeated allocation and deallocation on the heap. Second, for lists that could not be converted, a “scratchpad” approach was adopted—reusing the same list by manually clearing it between uses. These changes reduced the memory spike to about 100 MB per MCTS calculation, a 75% improvement compared to the original implementation.
